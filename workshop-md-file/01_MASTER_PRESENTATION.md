## 01 — Master Presentation Script

## "AI as an Administrative Officer's Digital Assistant" — 90-minute session for senior IAS Group A officers

Read this like a script, not an outline. Bracketed lines are stage directions. Everything else you can say close to verbatim, then make it your own voice.

## PHASE 1 — The Hook (0:00 – 0:05)

[Slide 1 is on screen before you speak: a single, large, photorealistic AI-generated image — no title, no logo, nothing that reveals it's synthetic. Suggested subject: a crowded Indian collectorate office, monsoon light through the windows, officers at work. See 03_LIVE_DEMOS.md for the exact prompt to generate this in advance.]

Stand beside the screen, not in front of it. Let the room look at the image for 5–6 seconds before you say anything.

"Before I say a single word — how many of you believe this is a real photograph?"

[Pause. Ask for a show of hands. Genuinely wait. If someone says "not real," ask them why — let them explain, don't correct them yet.]

"This photograph does not exist. There is no building, no officer, no camera. This entire image was generated by an AI model in about eight seconds, from a two-line instruction."

## [Pause for two full seconds. Let it land.]

"I'm not showing you this to impress you. I'm showing you this because if a machine can now produce something this convincing from a sentence — it can also draft your circulars, translate your notices, summarize your reports, and analyze your grievance data. That's what the next ninety minutes are about."

## PHASE 2 — Reframe (0:05 – 0:10)

## [Slide 2: title card — "AI as an Administrative Officer's Digital Assistant"]

"This session is not about ChatGPT. ChatGPT is one tool among many, and honestly, it isn't even the best tool for most of what you'll see today. This session is about a bigger shift: AI is no longer one general-purpose chatbot. It has split into specialists — one model for text, a different one for images, a different one for video, a different one for reading documents, a different one for code. Today I'll show you which specialist to reach for, for which job."

## [Slide 3: interactive poll — three questions, hands only, no explanation yet]

"Quick show of hands, no wrong answers: — Who here has used ChatGPT or a similar AI chatbot at least once? — Who has ever generated an image with AI? — Who has used AI for anything related to official work — a draft, a translation, a summary?"

[Note the pattern out loud — it's almost always "most people raise hands for #1, fewer for #2, very few for #3." Use that gap as your transition.]

"That gap — a lot of personal use, almost no official use — is exactly the gap this session exists to close."

## PHASE 3 — The AI Landscape (0:10 – 0:15)

[Slide 4: landscape grid — six columns: Text / Image / Video / Documents & Presentations / Code / Reasoning-Agents, each with 2–3 tool logos or names]


"Think of AI today the way you'd think of a Secretariat — you don't send every file to one desk. Text generation, image generation, video generation, document intelligence, and coding each have their own specialist tools. I'll show you all four of the governance-relevant ones live, in the next forty minutes. No theory yet — just watch."

## [Transition line into demos:]

"Let's start where every officer's day actually starts — with a piece of text you need to write, translate, and shorten, right now."

## [Transition into theory phase:]

"You've now seen four different specialists at work. Before we go further, I want to spend the next fifteen minutes making sure you can use these safely and effectively — not just be impressed by them."

## PHASE 5 — Concepts: Using AI Well (0:55 – 1:10)

## [Slide 9: Prompt Engineering]

"Every demo you just saw worked because of how it was asked, not just what tool was used. This is called prompt engineering, and it's the single highest-leverage skill in this room today."

Cover, briefly, using the rainfall circular as the running example each time (full explanations in 08_PROMPT_ENGINEERING.md ): - Give it a role: "You are a government press officer" changes the tone entirely. - Give it context and constraints: audience, length, tone, format. - Ask it to think step by step for anything analytical (e.g., "First list the key deadlines, then summarize each in one line"). - Iterate — the four follow-ups in the text demo were prompt engineering in action.

## [Slide 10: How this actually works, briefly — and where it goes wrong]

"Two things to always keep in mind. First: these tools work with 'tokens' — small chunks of text — and every conversation has a limit to how much it can hold in memory at once, called a context window. If you paste a very long document, sometimes it forgets the beginning by the end. Second, and more important: these tools can sound completely confident while being wrong. This is called hallucination."

"Never submit an AI-drafted number, date, legal citation, or factual claim into an official document without verifying it against a source. Use AI for drafting and structuring — not as the final word on facts."

[Full technical depth — tokens, context window, hallucination, RAG, reasoning models, agents, MCP — lives in 04_AI_KNOWLEDGE_GUIDE.md . Only use it if the room asks. Do not lecture on it unprompted.]

## PHASE 6 — Data Privacy (1:10 – 1:18)

## [Slide 11 — tone shifts here: serious, direct, no jokes]

"Everything so far has been about capability. Now the harder question: what should you never put into these tools?"

"Free, public AI tools — the ChatGPT, Gemini, and similar apps you and I have been using all morning — may use what you type to improve their models, unless you're on an enterprise or government-approved plan with that setting explicitly turned off. Treat every free AI tool the way you'd treat a phone call on an open line."

State clearly, slowly, and let it sit (full list in 06_DATA_PRIVACY.md ):

"Never upload: confidential government files, citizen Aadhaar or other identity data, police investigation records, Cabinet documents, internal financial reports, medical records, personal data of any citizen, vendor-confidential material, or tender evaluation files."

"For anything involving real citizen or government data, the answer isn't 'don't use AI' — it's 'use the right AI.' Enterprise AI subscriptions, on- premise/local models, and government-approved platforms exist precisely for this. I'll leave you the specifics in the handout."


## PHASE 7 — Deepfakes (1:18 – 1:25)

## [Slide 12 — tone stays serious]

"The same technology that generated our opening image can generate a fake voice note from a District Magistrate, a fake video of a minister, or a fake FIR document. This is not hypothetical — it is already happening in India, which is why the government notified new IT Rules amendments in February 2026 specifically to regulate this content."

Cover briefly (full detail in 07_DEEPFAKE_AND_AI_MISUSE.md ): - Platforms must now label AI-generated audio/visual content clearly and cannot let that label be removed. - Your personal checklist before you forward or act on anything: check the source, reverse-image-search unusual visuals, be suspicious of urgency ("forward immediately"), and verify through official channels before acting — never through the forwarded message itself.

"As officers, you will increasingly be the ones citizens turn to when something questionable is circulating. A thirty-second verification habit is the difference between stopping a rumor and amplifying one."

## PHASE 8 — Close and Q&A (1:25 – 1:30)

## [Slide 13: single line, large text]

"AI will not replace officers. Officers who understand AI will outperform officers who don't."

"Everything you saw today is free or nearly free, works in regional languages, and takes minutes to learn. The only real cost is the discipline to verify before you rely, and the judgment to know what never to share. That judgment — that's still entirely yours."

## [Slide 14: Q&A + handout note]

"I have a one-page handout with every tool, every prompt, and the data privacy checklist we covered. Let's open the floor."
